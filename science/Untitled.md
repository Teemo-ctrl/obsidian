- **Nucleotide Components:** A nucleotide is the basic structural unit of nucleic acids like DNA and RNA. It is composed of three distinct parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. These components link together to form the long backbone and coding sequence of genetic material.
    
      
    
- **Semi-Conservative Replication:** This model describes how DNA copies itself by separating its two parent strands to act as templates. Each newly formed DNA double helix contains one original, conserved parent strand and one entirely new daughter strand. This mechanism ensures genetic information is copied with high fidelity and fewer errors.
    
      
    
- **Enzymes in DNA Replication:** DNA replication relies on several specialized enzymes to unwind, copy, and seal the genetic code. Helicase unzips the double helix, primase lays down an RNA primer, and DNA polymerase builds the new complementary strand. Finally, ligase seals any gaps in the sugar-phosphate backbone to complete the process.
    
      
    
- **Leading and Lagging Strands:** Because DNA strands are antiparallel, DNA polymerase must synthesize them differently. The leading strand is built continuously in the same direction that the replication fork is opening. The lagging strand is built in the opposite direction in short, discontinuous segments known as Okazaki fragments.
    
      
    
- **Codon Definition:** A codon is a specific sequence of three consecutive nucleotides found on an mRNA molecule. This triplet code corresponds directly to a single, specific amino acid. Ribosomes read these codons during translation to determine the exact order of amino acids in a resulting protein.
    
      
    
- **Start and Stop Codons:** These specific codons act as the punctuation marks for the protein synthesis process. The start codon, AUG, signals the ribosome to begin translation and always brings the amino acid methionine first. Stop codons do not code for any amino acid and instead signal the ribosome to release the finished protein.
    
      
    
- **Gene Sequence to Protein:** The central dogma of molecular biology dictates that genetic information flows in one specific direction. The sequence of DNA nucleotides in a gene is first transcribed into an mRNA molecule. This mRNA sequence is then translated by ribosomes into a functional chain of amino acids, forming a protein.
    
      
    
- **Transcription Process:** Transcription is the first major step of gene expression where a specific DNA segment is copied into RNA. The enzyme RNA polymerase binds to the DNA, unwinds it, and builds a single-stranded mRNA molecule using complementary base pairing. This newly formed mRNA then carries the genetic blueprint out of the nucleus to the cytoplasm.
    
      
    
- **Introns and Exons:** In eukaryotic cells, newly transcribed pre-mRNA contains both coding and non-coding sequences. Introns are the non-coding segments that must be removed before translation can occur. Exons are the crucial coding sequences that remain and are spliced together to form the final, mature mRNA molecule.
    
      
    
- **Roles of RNA in Translation:** Three different types of RNA collaborate closely to synthesize proteins during translation. Messenger RNA provides the genetic instructions, while ribosomal RNA forms the structural core of the ribosome. Transfer RNA physically carries the correct amino acids to the ribosome by matching its anticodon to the mRNA codon.
    
      
    
- **Anticodon-Codon Pairing:** This pairing is the mechanism that ensures the correct amino acid is added to a growing protein chain. A tRNA molecule possesses a three-base sequence called an anticodon that is complementary to an mRNA codon. When these two sequences successfully bind through hydrogen bonding, the specific amino acid is transferred to the polypeptide.
    
      
    
- **Pre-mRNA Splicing:** After transcription, eukaryotic pre-mRNA undergoes a critical editing process before it can be translated. Specialized complexes called spliceosomes cut out the non-coding intron sequences from the RNA strand. The remaining coding exons are then fused together to create the mature, continuous mRNA transcript.
    
      
    
- **Gene Mutation Concept:** A gene mutation is a permanent, physical alteration in the nucleotide sequence of an organism's DNA. These changes can occur spontaneously during replication or be triggered by environmental factors like radiation and chemicals. While some mutations are harmless or even beneficial, others can disrupt protein function and cause genetic disorders.
    
      
    
- **Types of Mutations:** Mutations generally fall into categories based on how they alter the DNA sequence. Point mutations involve the substitution of a single nucleotide base, which might change only one amino acid. Frameshift mutations involve the insertion or deletion of bases, shifting the reading frame and completely altering the entire downstream protein structure.
    
      
    
- **Stages of Meiosis:** Meiosis is a specialized form of cell division that produces four haploid gametes from one diploid cell. It occurs in two distinct phases: Meiosis I separates homologous chromosome pairs, while Meiosis II separates sister chromatids. This reduction in chromosome number is essential for maintaining the correct amount of DNA during sexual reproduction.
    
      
    
- **Crossing Over:** Crossing over is a vital biological event that occurs during Prophase I of meiosis. Homologous chromosomes pair up closely and physically exchange corresponding segments of their DNA sequences. This swapping of genetic material creates unique combinations of alleles, driving genetic variation in offspring.
    
      
    
- **Pedigree Analysis:** A pedigree is a visual chart used to trace the inheritance of a specific trait through multiple generations of a family. By analyzing the patterns of affected and unaffected individuals, geneticists can determine if a trait is dominant, recessive, or sex-linked. This tool is frequently used in medicine to predict the probability of children inheriting genetic disorders.
    
      
    
- **Autosomal Linkage and Epistasis:** These are two advanced genetic concepts that complicate simple Mendelian inheritance ratios. Autosomal linkage occurs when different genes sit so close together on the same chromosome that they are usually inherited as a single unit. Epistasis occurs when the expression of one gene completely masks or modifies the phenotypic effect of a completely different gene.
    
      
    
- **Monohybrid Crosses:** A monohybrid cross is a genetic mating experiment focusing on how a single specific trait is inherited. When crossing two heterozygous parents, it typically results in a three-to-one phenotypic ratio in the offspring. This basic cross is fundamental for demonstrating Mendel's Law of Segregation.
    
      
    
- **Dihybrid Crosses:** A dihybrid cross tracks the inheritance patterns of two completely different, independent traits simultaneously. Crossing parents that are heterozygous for both traits usually yields a classic nine-to-three-to-three-to-one phenotypic ratio. This type of cross illustrates Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment.
    
      
    
- **Genotype and Phenotype:** These terms describe the relationship between an organism's genetic code and its physical traits. The genotype is the invisible, internal combination of alleles an organism carries for a specific gene. The phenotype is the observable physical manifestation of that genotype interacting with the environment.
    
      
    
- **Dominant and Recessive Trait:** These terms describe how different alleles of a gene interact with one another. A dominant trait is visibly expressed even if the individual only inherits one copy of that specific allele. A recessive trait remains hidden unless the individual inherits two identical copies of the recessive allele.
    
      
    
- **Chi-Squared Test:** This is a statistical tool used to determine if the difference between observed biological data and expected data is due to chance. Biologists calculate a chi-squared value and compare it to a critical value table based on degrees of freedom. If the calculated value is too high, it indicates that an outside factor, rather than random chance, is affecting the results.
    
      
    
- **TYR Gene and Albinism:** The TYR gene provides the blueprint for tyrosinase, an enzyme crucial for producing melanin pigment in the skin, hair, and eyes. If an individual inherits two mutated, recessive copies of this gene, they cannot produce melanin properly. This results in the genetic condition known as albinism, characterized by a distinct lack of pigmentation.
    
      
    
- **F8 Gene and Hemophilia:** The F8 gene is responsible for creating a vital blood-clotting protein called coagulation factor eight. Because this gene is located on the X chromosome, mutations primarily affect males who only have one X chromosome. A defective F8 gene leads to Hemophilia A, a disorder where blood fails to clot properly after injuries.
    
      
    
- **Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Regulation:** Gene regulation determines when and how genes are turned on or off in different organisms. Prokaryotes use simple operon systems to quickly adapt to environmental changes by regulating groups of genes together. Eukaryotes utilize much more complex mechanisms, including transcription factors and chromatin modifications, to tightly control gene expression in specialized cells.
    
      
    
- **Replication, Transcription, Translation (Essay Concept):** DNA replication must first copy the genetic code perfectly before cell division can occur. Transcription then takes a specific section of this DNA and converts it into a mobile messenger RNA molecule. Finally, translation reads this mRNA at the ribosome to link amino acids together into a functional, finished protein.
    
      
    
- **Purines and Pyrimidines:** These are the two categories of nitrogenous bases that make up the steps of the DNA and RNA ladders. Purines, which include adenine and guanine, have a larger double-ring molecular structure. Pyrimidines, which include cytosine, thymine, and uracil, have a smaller single-ring structure, and they uniquely pair with purines to maintain structural stability.
    
      
    
- **Codominant Alleles (Blood Type Essay):** In codominance, neither allele completely masks the other, leading to both being distinctly expressed in the phenotype. A perfect example is the human ABO blood group system, where the A and B alleles are fully codominant. If an individual inherits both, they will have type AB blood, expressing both A and B antigens equally on their red blood cells.
    
      
    
- **Sex-Linked Trait (Haemophilia/Colorblindness Essay):** Sex-linked traits are governed by genes located specifically on the sex chromosomes, most commonly the X chromosome. Because males only possess one X chromosome, they will definitively express any recessive disorder located on it. Females must inherit two defective X chromosomes to show the trait, making these conditions significantly rarer in women.
    
      
    
- **Solving for Chi-Squared Test (Essay Concept):** To solve this in an essay, you first state a null hypothesis asserting that any difference between expected and observed results is random. You then calculate the expected genetic ratios, plug both expected and observed numbers into the chi-squared formula, and solve. Finally, you determine your degrees of freedom and check a critical value table to see if you must reject or accept the hypothesis.
